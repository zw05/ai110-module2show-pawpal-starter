# Dental Care

_General guidance, not veterinary advice. Check with your vet for your pet's specific needs._

## Brushing
Brush with pet-safe toothpaste, never human toothpaste. Daily is best, and a few times a week still helps. Start slowly, letting your pet taste the paste first, then build up to short sessions.

## Dental care by age
- **Puppies and kittens:** get used to mouth handling early. They lose baby teeth between about 3 and 7 months. Offer safe chew toys and never pull a loose tooth.
- **Adults:** most dental disease begins by age 3. Brush regularly and book a yearly dental check.
- **Seniors:** are more prone to gum disease and tooth loss. Soft or moistened food may help if chewing is painful.

## Chews and diet
Dental chews and dental diets can reduce plaque, but they do not replace brushing. Avoid very hard items such as bones, antlers or hard nylon toys that can crack teeth. A good test is that if you could not press a thumbnail into it, it is probably too hard.

## Professional cleaning
A vet can scale and polish teeth under anesthesia, and check below the gum line. Small dogs and flat-faced breeds are especially prone to dental problems.

## Should I take my dog or cat to the dentist?
Pets see a veterinarian for dental care, not a human dentist. Many vet clinics offer a dental exam and cleaning, and some have a veterinary dentist. A healthy dog or cat should have its teeth checked at least once a year at its regular checkup, and the vet will say if a professional dental cleaning is needed. A 1-year-old dog usually just needs a routine exam and a start on regular tooth brushing. Go sooner if you notice bad breath, red or bleeding gums, a broken or loose tooth, drooling, or trouble eating.

## How often is a dental cleaning needed
Most adult pets need a professional cleaning every 1 to 3 years, depending on breed, age and how much tartar builds up. Small dogs and flat-faced breeds often need them more often. Daily brushing at home can stretch the time between cleanings. Cleanings are done under anesthesia so the vet can scale and polish the teeth safely, take X-rays and check below the gum line. Ask the vet whether pre-anesthetic blood work is advised, especially for older pets.

## Warning signs
Bad breath, red or bleeding gums, drooling, dropping food, pawing at the mouth or reluctance to eat can mean dental disease. See a vet. Most pets benefit from a professional dental check once a year.
