# Dog Training

_General guidance, not veterinary advice. Check with your vet for your pet's specific needs._

## Basics
Keep sessions short, 5 to 10 minutes, once or twice a day. Reward the behavior you want right away with a small treat, praise or play. Consistency matters more than session length, and every family member should use the same cues.

## Training by age
- **Puppies (8 weeks to 6 months):** have a short attention span. Teach name, sit, come, and settling calmly. Start crate and handling practice early. Their best learning window for socialization is before about 14 weeks.
- **Adolescents (6 to 18 months):** often seem to forget their training and test limits. This is normal. Stay consistent, keep rewarding, and practice in new places with distractions.
- **Adults:** keep learning well. Use new tricks, scent games and sports to stay mentally sharp.
- **Seniors:** can still learn. Use gentle, low-impact cues and food rewards, and add hand signals if hearing declines.

## Housetraining
Take a puppy out after waking, eating, playing and every 1 to 2 hours. A rough guide is that a puppy can hold it for about one hour per month of age, plus one, up to a limit of around 8 hours. Reward in the right spot straight away. Clean accidents with an enzyme cleaner and never punish a dog for them. Regression around 4 to 6 months or adolescence is common.

## Crate training
A crate should be a comfortable, safe den, never a punishment. Feed meals in it and build up the time slowly. Do not crate a puppy for longer than it can hold its bladder.

## Leash walking
Stop walking when the leash goes tight and move on when it slackens. Reward the dog for staying beside you. Use a flat collar or a front-clip harness, and avoid retractable leashes in busy places.

## Biting and chewing
Puppies mouth and chew while teething, from about 3 to 6 months. Redirect to a chew toy, stop play when teeth touch skin, and keep valuables out of reach. Frozen chew toys can soothe sore gums.

## Separation anxiety
Practice short absences and build up slowly. Leave a food puzzle or chew toy. Keep arrivals and departures calm. If your dog destroys things, howls or panics when alone, ask a vet or a certified trainer for help.

## What to avoid
Do not use yelling, hitting or harsh corrections. They damage trust and often make behavior problems worse.
